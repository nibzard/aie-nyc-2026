# AI and the Future of Fundamental Investing

> In a Kepler-hosted fireside, former Citadel PM and Candlestick Capital founder Jack Woodruff argues that AI will not change long-term stock picking or divergent-view alpha, but will flatten analyst prep work and turn investing into a time-management game where humans stay the bottleneck.

## TL;DR
- Long-term stock picking will not change much: there has always been a mosaic, and alpha comes from divergent views. That stays.
- What changes completely is table stakes: what everybody has, and how they synthesize information. Traditional portfolio manager and analyst roles get "completely thrown upon their heads."
- Humans are still the bottleneck. Information must still get into your head, so the open question is how man and machine work together.
- AI compresses prep work that took six to eight hours into about five minutes. The freed time goes to proprietary research, channel checks, and industry backdrop work.
- Reading 10-Ks and transcripts will not disappear for analysts truly close to a name. Big-data grinding will be outsourced.
- The winners are the people who use these tools to spend the right amount of time on the right names.

## Key lessons
1. Split the question "what changes" in two. Long-term stock picking itself will not change, because the mosaic and divergent-view alpha creation have always existed and stay the same [05:56:50]. What changes completely is how people synthesize information and where table stakes land [05:57:09].
2. Expect role upheaval, but do not expect a clear blueprint yet. Woodruff says the traditional roles of portfolio manager and analyst "are going to be completely thrown upon their heads," yet it is too early to say what the new roles look like because the tools are so new to users [05:57:23, 05:57:31].
3. Humans remain the constraint. At the end of the day you have to get the information into your head, so the design problem is how man and machine work together [05:58:12, 05:58:24].
4. Treat investment management as a time management exercise. One stock gets world-class knowledge but no action; spread time across more names and you hit diminishing returns per name [05:58:32 to 05:58:55].
5. Quantify the AI saving concretely: preparation work that used to take six to eight hours can now take about five minutes with an AI program [05:59:11]. That raise in table stakes frees time for proprietary research, channel checks, and industry backdrop work [05:59:51].
6. Do the headcount math against competition, not in isolation. His career data points: 18 people covering 400 stocks at Citadel (Surveyor) and 30 stock pickers at Candlestick [05:58:02]. AI "perhaps" helps cover more names, but if you finish in two hours, a competitor still works 16 hours a day to find the extra edge [05:59:36, 06:00:04].
7. Sort junior analyst work into two buckets. Reading 10-Ks and transcripts stays, because analysts truly close to a name must still process that information; summaries only cut the time. Going through big data gets outsourced [06:00:45 to 06:01:17].
8. Redefine the analyst job as triage. The AI or agent pushes you toward the right names, and the winners are the people who use the tools to spend the right amount of time hunting a divergent view [06:01:27, 06:01:43].

## Session narrative
Vinoo Ganesh, CEO of Kepler, opened the fireside at 2:43:50 PM and brought Jack Woodruff to the stage. Woodruff introduced himself as the founder and former Chief Investment Officer of Candlestick Capital Management, a long-short equity hedge fund in Greenwich, Connecticut. Before that, he was a partner and portfolio manager at Surveyor Capital, a division of Citadel. Ganesh framed the format: the two had held this conversation before, and he called Woodruff one of the most enlightening people to talk with, because Woodruff spent his career picking stocks while the environment changed faster than ever.

Ganesh's first question asked what fundamental investing looks like in the age of AI, five years from now. Woodruff split the answer. Long-term stock picking will not change much, because the mosaic and divergent-view alpha have always existed. What changes completely is the synthesis layer: where table stakes go, what everybody has, and how they process data. He predicted the traditional portfolio manager and analyst roles would be upended, but he refused to draw the new org chart. The tools are too new to users, and nobody has proven the endpoint yet.

The core of the chat turned to capacity. Ganesh cited the numbers from Woodruff's career: 18 people covering 400 stocks at Citadel, and 30 stock pickers at Candlestick. Could one great analyst cover all 400 by 2030? Woodruff answered that humans are still the bottleneck, because information must still get into your head. He framed investment management as a time management exercise, with diminishing returns per name as coverage widens. AI can perhaps raise coverage, and it can compress six to eight hours of preparation into five minutes. But he warned against reading that as a headcount story: the industry is competitive, so a rival who works 16 hours a day will absorb the same tools, and the work you paid an analyst for a year or two ago is not what you will pay an analyst to do in a year.

On junior analyst work, Ganesh asked what would look absurd in five years. Woodruff pushed back on the popular take that analysts stop reading 10-Ks and transcripts. If you are truly close to a name, you still need to process that information; summaries only cut the time. Big-data grinding, by contrast, is easy to outsource. The agent's real job is triage: pushing you toward the right names, since the winners are the people who spend the right amount of time hunting the divergent view. ## Speaker details
**Jack Woodruff** - Senior Advisor, Kepler. Founder and former Chief Investment Officer of Candlestick Capital Management (CCM); he led the firm from 2019 through 2025 and built its investment, research, and risk frameworks. Before that he was a portfolio manager in Citadel's equity unit, Surveyor Capital, from 2010 to 2019, where he built and led an 18-person team and invested in more than 400 global equities. He also worked at Sigma Capital (a division of SAC Capital Advisors), Northsound Capital, and Trivium Capital; he began at Ernst & Young and holds an MBA in Finance from NYU Stern and a B.S. in Accounting from USC.

**Vinoo Ganesh** - CEO, Kepler. CEO of Kepler, the ground truth platform for AI, starting with finance: language models interpret financial questions and plan analysis, while deterministic software retrieves source data, runs calculations, and records how every result was produced. He spent seven years at Palantir, was CTO of Veraset (acquired after reaching $15M ARR), and was Head of Business Engineering at Citadel. Website: https://www.kepler.ai
