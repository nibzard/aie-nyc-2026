# Everyone Has AI. Who Has the Advantage?

> With AI access now universal, Sid Pednekar argues that advantage comes from judgment plus three pillars: design the right problem, build shared capabilities, and compound the advantage.

## TL;DR
- Only about 37% of surveyed enterprises report a positive EBIT contribution from AI, and only about 6% qualify as high performers (about 5% EBIT contribution). Access is widespread; impact is uneven.
- In an era where intelligence has become cheap and abundant, the scarce resource is judgment: choosing what matters and how to act when there is no single obvious answer.
- Leading firms obsess over the right problem instead of the tool. Company A asks "why are our material signals reaching us too late?"; Company B bolts a summarization copilot onto the existing process — a "faster horse" that loses context.
- Leaders concentrate more than 80% of AI investment in a few high-value, end-to-end workflows, and they redesign work rather than just automate it (75% of high performers redesign workflows versus 25% of the rest).
- Shared capabilities matter: converge technology and business roles, standardize the core and personalize the edges, and modernize infrastructure built for humans, not agents (71% of organizations are modernizing; 23% invest 6 to 10% of annual revenue in it).
- Advantage compounds when individual learnings — with their evidence — are fed back into the system as tools, context, and monitoring flywheels.

## Key lessons
1. Quantify the AI gap before planning. Recent studies show only about 37% of surveyed enterprises see a positive EBIT contribution from AI, and only about 6% qualify as high performers (about 5% EBIT contribution). Earlier-year studies show about 66% report productivity gains, but only about 40% and 20% see cost reductions and revenue growth.
2. Treat judgment as the scarce asset. Intelligence (software, analytics, recommendations, reports) is now abundant, so more answers do not create more value. Judgment — the creativity to pick possibilities, the taste to know what good looks like, the weighing of trade-offs — is finite. The core question: where is our best judgment worth spending?
3. Start with the problem, not the tool. Company B asks "where can we add AI to the analyst's work?" and pipes earnings transcripts through a summarization firehose. Company A asks "why are our material signals reaching us too late?" and "where is our bottleneck?" Studies show leaders spend attention on fewer, higher-quality decisions.
4. Concentrate investment in the highest-value workflows. Company B launches the most AI pilots (a summarization bot here, a copilot there, a data retrieval bot here). Company A aligns engineering, analytics, AI capacity, and judgment toward end-to-end research pipelines. Research shows leaders put more than 80% of investment into these high-value use cases. Separate real value generation from innovation theater and productivity theater.
5. Redesign the work, do not just automate it. Company B speeds up summaries, model updates, and recommendations to the PM. Company A has the analyst define an investment thesis, then monitors continuously for evidence that refutes or supports it, investigates exceptions, and deep-dives select cases. High performers actually redesign workflows at 75% versus 25% for the rest.
6. Standardize the core and personalize the edges. Example: replace one-off data-science workflows with a shared core of data sets, semantic layers, and knowledge of trade-offs, gaps, and what worked. Each new person becomes a plug-and-play module that stands on the shoulders of predecessors. Ask: what should the next person not have to rebuild?
7. Modernize infrastructure for agents. The enterprise was built for one human interacting with one application; agents take context, deliberate, and act across workflows and applications. Studies show 71% of organizations are modernizing core infrastructure for AI, and 23% invest 6 to 10% of annual revenue in core system modernization. Data quality and data reuse are the common challenges.
8. Build feedback loops that compound. Ask of every failure: what can we learn that gives us an edge? Ask of every success: how do we make it part of the system? The test is "the individual learned but did the system?" Feed corrections back into the org as new tools or context, and preserve the evidence along with the learnings.

## Session narrative
Dr. Sid Pednekar opened by framing the talk around one question: organizations all have access to the same AI, so who actually gets the advantage? He introduced himself as an AI technology and data executive in asset management, with tenure in hedge funds, investment, and academic research, where he led strategy and built specialized teams across three verticals: optimizing efficiency, driving P&L, and reducing costs. He then set up the talk's running device: two hypothetical asset-management companies, Company A and Company B, whose research-analyst workflows the audience follows for the rest of the talk. The session ran in the livestream's afternoon block (3:16:49 PM to 3:34:48 PM ET), earlier than the 3:45 PM slot the published agenda listed.

The lay of the land, per recent studies he cited, is uneven impact: only about 37% of surveyed enterprises see positive EBIT contribution from AI, only about 6% are high performers (about 5% EBIT contribution), and earlier studies show 66% claiming productivity gains but only 40% and 20% seeing cost reductions and revenue growth. From there he introduced the cross-cutting idea and the three pillars. The cross-cutting idea: intelligence has become cheap, so in an abundant world the scarce resource is judgment — ubiquitous across the C-suite, software engineers, AI leaders, portfolio managers, and data scientists — and since judgment is finite, the question is where to spend it.

Pillar one, design the right problem, carried the bulk of the talk. Company B starts with the tool: it finds a summarization workflow, pipes earnings transcripts through a firehose, and calls faster summaries a win. Pednekar called this a "faster horse" that loses context through information reduction. Company A instead redesigns the workflow: it asks why material signals arrive late, where the bottleneck is, and where to focus attention. He cited research that leaders concentrate attention on fewer, higher-quality decisions, put more than 80% of investment into high-value use cases, and redesign rather than just automate (75% versus 25% for high performers versus the rest), warning against innovation theater and productivity theater. His worked example: Company A has the analyst define an investment thesis and then continuously monitors evidence that refutes or supports it, investigates exceptions, and deep-dives select cases.

Pillar two, build shared capabilities, covered the convergence of technology and business — forward-deployed engineers, forward-deployed product managers, and the inverse, forward-deployed portfolio managers — plus breaking down silos. He advocated standardizing the core and personalizing the edges, with a data-science shared core (data sets, semantic layers, trade-offs, gaps) that makes each new person plug-and-play. Because the enterprise was built for humans on single applications while agents cross workflows, he cited 71% of organizations modernizing core infrastructure and 23% investing 6 to 10% of annual revenue in it, with data quality and reuse as the common challenge. He sketched team agents as the reusable-knowledge layer: expert agents as subject-matter experts, workflow agents like Slack bots that open and close tickets, and chief-of-staff agents that track organizational state. Pillar three, compound the advantage, closed the argument: leaders mine failures for an edge, make successes part of the system, and ask whether the system (not just the individual) learned, preserving evidence with learnings and running an AI-first capacity flywheel around monitored metrics. He ended where he began: everyone has AI — who has the advantage?
