Runway co-founder Cristóbal Valenzuela argues that next-frame prediction, scaled far enough, turns video models into universal simulation engines, and demos Continuum, a video-model operating system that Runway plans to release within 8 to 12 months.

## TL;DR
- Runway's first AI film, "The Orange" (2019), came from a 128x128-pixel model force-upsampled to video. Valenzuela says today's photoreal state of the art will look just as primitive in a few years.
- Scaled next-frame prediction learns physics, object permanence, gravity, and occlusion from video alone. Runway's simulation-style environments have no coded physics laws — the model learns behavior "by brute force of scale".
- Runway measures whether people can tell real video from generated video. Valenzuela says that Turing test for video is already passed, and benchmarks now weigh physics and geometry, not only taste.
- A "world interface model" turns any image into a clickable, interactive application with no Unity, Unreal, or Blender scene behind it.
- Runway is building a robotics lab so video models can drive autonomous, embodied systems.
- Continuum is an operating system whose screen is streamed by a video model: interactive "portals" cut from web content, a memory of past visualizations, and coding agents that reason visually. Most of it ships to everyone in 8 to 12 months.

## Key lessons
1. Cinema's 120-year-old trick is the core of the research. Video is an optical illusion: stitch frames fast enough and you get motion. Runway applies the same idea — next-frame prediction — with large networks and heavy compute, and rides scaling laws that it believes hold for video just as they do for language.
2. Emergent understanding comes free with scale. Predicting the next frame forces the model to learn physics, object permanence, gravity, occlusion, and how objects relate in a scene (his example: a camel standing on a ball, water in a container). Runway evaluates models on physics- and geometry-based benchmarks, not only on whether people like the output.
3. Real-versus-generated perception is measured and, per Valenzuela, already solved: "I look at that video, and I've been doing this for 10 years, I can't tell you if that's real or not" [08:33:52 | 5:14:27 PM]; the transcript renders the claim as "that touring test we already passed".
4. Simulation emerges without physics laws. In game-like, playable environments generated by the model, Runway wrote no rules for object behavior — "The model just knows how to do that by brute force of scale" [08:35:54 | 5:16:29 PM]. Everything shown before the Continuum segment took minutes or seconds to render, so interactivity and real-time speed are the frontier.
5. Any pixel can become an interface. A "world interface model" — a video model trained to understand actions and clicks — made a single car image interactive: turn the lights, rotate the car, open the hood. There is no 3D representation: no Unity, no Unreal, no Blender. A second demo moved objects in a room from just an image.
6. Video models are becoming robot brains. Runway is spinning up an entire robotics lab; its New York office is half Hollywood studio, half robots walking around. The same world models are intended to power how autonomous, embodied systems walk and reason.
7. Continuum treats the operating system itself as a real-time video model. Selected web content becomes a "portal" — a fully interactive real-time video. Past portals form a memory ("the disk of the imaginations"). Resizing an application changes the experience. Searching for Mars made Continuum generate a Mars outpost interface in real time, seeded with data on robots currently on Mars and missions projected for 2030.
8. Agents can think visually, and extrapolation is a survival skill. Continuum connected to a coding agent lets the agent "think through" a problem visually (for example, a 3D model of a bike) instead of only via code, and lets humans guide the agent better than text alone. Valenzuela also argues that extrapolating the next 6, 12, 18 months is one of the most important skills in AI and company building today.

## Session narrative
A stage voiceover opens the final keynote of the day: "What if AI could do more than generate content and actually simulate the physical world?" It welcomes Cristóbal Valenzuela, co-founder and co-CEO of Runway. He opens with a joke — "Welcome to my talk about the Matrix and how we're living through it" — and grounds himself in place and time: almost a decade at Runway, started in New York about 10 years ago, building the company and its research lab there.

The argument starts with history. He screens Runway's first AI film, "The Orange" (2019), built on an image model that generated 128x128 pixels and was forcefully upsampled. He calls it art now, "like looking at a film from the 1900s". Then he cuts to state-of-the-art generated video: no real characters, no real locations. The point of the pairing is humility about the present — he still believes today's output "will look like The Orange in just a couple more years".

The core claim follows. Cinema discovered next-frame prediction 120 to 130 years ago; Runway scales that idea with big networks and heavy compute, backed by scaling laws the team believes apply to video. Predicting frames forces the model to learn physics, object permanence, gravity, occlusion, and object behavior. Runway measures whether humans can distinguish real from generated video and concludes that test is passed. Pushed into game-like environments, the model becomes a simulation system with no physics laws written by anyone — behavior learned "by brute force of scale". He flags the frontier clearly: everything shown so far took minutes or seconds to generate, not real time.

The back half is a demo arc. First, a "world interface model": one car image becomes an interactive application — lights on, car rotated, hood opened — with no Unity, Unreal, or Blender underneath. A second demo moves objects in a room from a single image. He also reveals a new robotics lab: half the New York office looks like a Hollywood studio, half is robots, with video models intended as the brains of autonomous, embodied systems. Then the closing extreme: Continuum, a research-stage operating system whose screen is streamed by a video model. He turns a fog-data website about San Francisco into an interactive "portal", browses a memory of earlier portals (batteries), resizes a photosynthesis app to get a different experience, and searches for Mars, which makes Continuum generate a live, explorable Mars outpost seeded with real rover data. Last, "visual thinking": a coding agent spins up Continuum to reason through problems visually, so a person can guide it better than through text alone. He closes by saying that when he says everything will be simulated, he means that this should feel obvious in a few years — and that Continuum may one day look like The Orange does now. Most of what he showed ships to everyone within 8 to 12 months.
